# LDO Cross-Reference: What Actually Has to Match

> **Author**: FPGACenter Sourcing Team
> **Reading time**: ~17 minutes
> **Topics**: LDO, linear regulator, cross-reference, second source, stability

---

<img src="/uploads/ldo-cross-reference-guide-hero.jpg" alt="Macro view of an LDO regulator chip on a blue PCB, illustrating what specifications must match for cross-referencing." width="1200" height="630" fetchpriority="high" />

**An LDO cross-reference is only a candidate list.** Cross-reference tools match output voltage, output current, package and sometimes dropout — roughly a third of what determines whether a linear regulator works in your circuit. This guide covers the parameters that decide the outcome, in the order they most often cause failures, with the arithmetic you need to check each one.

## Key takeaways

- **Output capacitor stability is the number one cause of failed LDO substitutions.** Older PNP-based regulators need ESR; modern ceramic-stable parts need low ESR. The two are not interchangeable.
- Ceramic **DC bias derating** can halve effective capacitance, putting a design outside the stable range while the BOM shows margin.
- Quiescent current varies by **four orders of magnitude** between otherwise similar parts. If the design has a standby mode, this is usually why the original was chosen.
- Dropout is quoted at maximum load and 25 °C. Check it at **minimum input voltage, maximum load and minimum temperature** instead.
- Package substitution changes thermal resistance even when the current rating is unchanged. Compute dissipation; do not trust the headline number.
- If the input-to-output differential has grown, the correct answer may be a **switching regulator**, not another LDO.

## The comparison table lies by omission

Type an obsolete LDO part number into any cross-reference tool and you get a list. That list is generated by matching a handful of indexed fields. The parameters that actually decide the outcome — output capacitor stability range, quiescent current at your load, PSRR at the frequency that matters, enable threshold, thermal behaviour in the real package — are rarely indexed at all.

So the tool returns a dozen "equivalents". Perhaps three work without changes, several work with a different output capacitor, and at least one oscillates.

Linear regulators are the largest analog family in our catalogue at roughly 67,900 part numbers. Finding a candidate is not the problem. Knowing which candidates are real is the problem.

This is the checklist we work through when quoting an LDO substitution, ordered by how often each item is the thing that breaks.

## 1. Output capacitor stability: the number one failure

<img src="/uploads/ldo-regulator-multimeter-testing-validation.jpg" alt="Engineer testing a surface mount voltage regulator on a PCB with multimeter probes." width="800" height="450" loading="lazy" />

A linear regulator is a feedback loop and the output capacitor sits inside it. The regulator's internal compensation is designed around an assumed range of capacitance and equivalent series resistance. Move outside that range and phase margin collapses.

Three generations of LDO are in circulation, with incompatible expectations:

| Generation | Pass element | Expects | Typical output cap | Symptom when mismatched |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Classic | PNP / NPN | ESR for a stabilising zero | Tantalum, ~0.1–2 Ω | Oscillates with low-ESR ceramic |
| Modern | PMOS | Low ESR | X5R/X7R ceramic, mΩ | Can be unstable with high ESR |
| "Any-cap" | PMOS + internal compensation | Wide tolerance | Either | Usually fine — still verify the range |

A classic part and a modern part can share output voltage, current, dropout and package, and be listed as equivalents. Swapping one for the other without changing the output capacitor is the most common way an LDO substitution fails, and it typically fails *intermittently*, at temperature, on a fraction of units, which is the worst failure mode to debug.

### The ESR "stability box"

Older datasheets often print a stability region: a plot of ESR against load current, with a shaded band inside which the loop is stable. It has both an upper and a lower boundary. The lower boundary is the trap — engineers reflexively treat lower ESR as better. It is not.

If the original datasheet shows such a plot and the replacement does not, that difference alone tells you the two parts use different compensation strategies.

### Ceramic DC bias derating

This catches experienced teams. A multilayer ceramic capacitor loses capacitance as DC bias rises:

| Nominal | Case size | Approximate effective value at 3.3 V bias |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 10 µF X5R | 0603 | often 4–5 µF |
| 10 µF X5R | 0805 | often 6–7 µF |
| 22 µF X5R | 0805 | often 10–13 µF |

Exact figures depend on the specific capacitor: the point is the magnitude of the effect, not these numbers. If a datasheet requires a minimum of 4.7 µF and your nominal 10 µF 0603 delivers about 4 µF at 3.3 V, you are outside the stable range while the BOM claims 2× margin.

Temperature compounds it: X5R falls off at both extremes, so a design marginally stable at 25 °C may not be at −40 °C.

**Check:** find the recommended output capacitance *and* ESR range in both datasheets and confirm they overlap at your actual operating point after bias and temperature derating. If they do not overlap, the substitution requires a capacitor change, which means it is not a drop-in.

## 2. Quiescent current

If the original LDO was chosen for a battery-powered or always-on design, quiescent current was probably the reason. Linear regulators span roughly four orders of magnitude here.

| Class | Typical Iq | Typical use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nanopower | 0.5–3 µA | Coin cell, always-on standby rail |
| Low power | 10–50 µA | Battery-powered instrumentation |
| General purpose | 50–500 µA | Mains-powered, no standby budget |
| Classic bipolar | 3–10 mA | Legacy designs, high dropout tolerance |

Three numbers to separate:

- **Ground current at load.** Bipolar architectures draw ground current roughly proportional to load; PMOS designs are nearly flat. For a design idling most of its life, the light-load figure governs.
- **Shutdown current.** Usually specified at 25 °C only. Leakage rises materially at high ambient: a part quoted at 0.1 µA may draw several microamps at 85 °C.
- **Iq versus dropout trade-off.** Nanopower parts generally have worse dropout and worse PSRR. These axes trade against each other by physics, not by vendor choice.

**Check:** quiescent current at your load, and shutdown current at your maximum ambient, not the headline conditions.

## 3. Dropout at the current you actually draw

Dropout voltage is quoted at a specific load, usually the maximum, and usually at 25 °C. It is not a constant.

A part quoted at 300 mV dropout at 500 mA drops far less at 50 mA, which is helpful. But dropout generally *increases* at low temperature, which is not.

The binding question is whether the regulator stays in regulation at minimum input and maximum load, at minimum temperature. Worked example for a 3.3 V rail from a nominal 5 V supply:

```
Vin(min) = 5.0 V nominal
         − 5%  supply tolerance      = 4.75 V
         − 0.3 V upstream drop        = 4.45 V
Headroom = 4.45 − 3.3 = 1.15 V
```

1.15 V of headroom is comfortable for almost any LDO. Now the same rail from a single-cell Li-ion at end of discharge:

```
Vin(min) = 3.4 V  (cell near empty)
Headroom = 3.4 − 3.3 = 0.10 V = 100 mV
```

At 100 mV headroom the choice of LDO is dictated entirely by dropout, and most general-purpose parts are eliminated. A substitution that ignores this produces a product that works until the battery is half flat.

**Check:** compute worst-case input, then confirm dropout at maximum load and minimum temperature.

## 4. Thermal — arithmetic that does not care about the rating

<img src="/uploads/smd-package-sizes-footprint-comparison.jpg" alt="Top-down view comparing various tiny SMD component packages like SOT-23 and DFN to highlight footprint matching." width="800" height="450" loading="lazy" />

For a linear regulator every volt dropped becomes heat:

```
P = (Vin − Vout) × Iload
```

Worked example: 5 V to 3.3 V at 500 mA.

```
P = (5.0 − 3.3) × 0.5 = 0.85 W
```

Junction temperature rise depends entirely on package and board:

| Package | Typical θJA on decent copper | Rise at 0.85 W | Tj at 25 °C ambient | Tj at 85 °C ambient |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| SOT-23-5 | ~200 °C/W | ~170 °C | 195 °C ✗ | far over ✗ |
| SOT-223 | ~60 °C/W | ~51 °C | 76 °C ✓ | 136 °C ✗ |
| DPAK | ~35 °C/W | ~30 °C | 55 °C ✓ | 115 °C ✗ |

Both the SOT-23 and the SOT-223 versions may carry the same "500 mA" headline rating. Only one of them can deliver it in this application, and neither survives 85 °C ambient at this dissipation.

This is why package substitutions quietly fail. When a replacement moves from SOT-223 to a smaller "compatible" package, the derating curve moves with it.

Also confirm behaviour at the limit: current limit may be constant-current (the part heats until thermal shutdown) or foldback/hiccup (it retries). A design relying on the original's fault behaviour may respond differently.

**Check:** dissipation at worst case; θJA for the specific package on a board like yours; junction temperature at maximum ambient; current-limit and thermal-shutdown style.

## 5. PSRR and output noise, where the rail feeds something sensitive

If the LDO exists to clean a rail before an ADC reference, an RF section, a PLL or a sensor front end, rejection and noise are functional requirements.

PSRR is strongly frequency-dependent. A front-page claim of 70 dB is typically measured at 100 Hz or 1 kHz. At the switching frequency of the buck converter feeding it — commonly 500 kHz to 2.2 MHz — rejection may be 30 dB or less. Two LDOs quoting identical headline PSRR can differ by 20 dB where it matters.

A concrete way to frame it: if the upstream buck produces 20 mVpp of ripple at 1 MHz and your ADC reference tolerates 200 µVpp, you need 40 dB of rejection *at 1 MHz*. That is a curve-reading exercise, not a headline-number exercise.

Output noise (usually µVRMS over 10 Hz to 100 kHz) is a separate axis, and it trades against quiescent current.

**Check:** PSRR curves at the upstream switching frequency; noise over the bandwidth the load cares about. If the datasheet gives only a single number, treat that as a reason to bench-test.

## 6. Control pins and start-up behaviour

Easy to overlook because they are rarely tabulated:

| Pin / behaviour | What varies | Consequence if it differs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Enable polarity | Active-high vs active-low | Rail permanently on or permanently off |
| Enable threshold | Logic-level vs fraction of Vin | Will not turn on from a low-voltage GPIO |
| Enable abs max | May or may not tolerate > Vin | Damage during sequencing |
| Soft-start | Fixed, programmable, or none | Sequencing window missed; or inrush too high |
| Output discharge | Present or absent on disable | Rail collapses too fast or too slowly |
| Power-good | Threshold, polarity, deglitch | Downstream supervisor misfires |

**Check:** list every pin on the original and confirm the substitute's behaviour on each, not merely that a pin exists in the same position.

## 7. Package and pinout — verify, never assume

Package names are not pinout guarantees. SOT-23-5 LDOs exist with several different pin assignments; enable and ground in particular move between families. "SOT-23-5 LDO" is not one thing.

Confirm the pinout drawing, not the package name. Then confirm the thermal pad: some packages require the exposed pad to be soldered for the current rating to hold, and a substitute without one (or with one tied to a different net internally) is neither mechanically nor electrically interchangeable.

## A worked shortlist

Suppose an older 3.3 V fixed regulator has gone end-of-life. The catalogue offers plenty of candidates: a classic bipolar part such as `NCP1117DT33RKG`, a low-noise CMOS device such as `TPS73250DBVR`, a nanopower part such as `MIC5365-2.7YMT-TZ`, an automotive-qualified option such as `TPS71715QDRVRQ1`.

These four are not interchangeable with one another in any meaningful sense. They differ by design in pass element, stability requirements, quiescent current and package. Which is correct depends entirely on the questions above, and the right first move is to answer those questions about *your* circuit before looking at any list.

Always confirm the current datasheet for whichever part you shortlist; parameters get revised across silicon revisions, and an application note written for revision A may not describe revision C.

## When a linear regulator is the wrong answer

If a substitution is being forced by obsolescence, ask whether the rail should still be linear at all. Dropping 3 V at 500 mA dissipates 1.5 W that a small buck converter would largely eliminate. If the original LDO was chosen when the input rail sat closer to the output, and the input has since changed, a switcher may now be the better engineering answer — see [replacing a discontinued DC-DC regulator](/blog/dc-dc-regulator-replacement-guide) for what that involves.

Conversely, if the rail feeds something noise-sensitive, keeping it linear and accepting the heat is frequently correct. An LDO downstream of a buck (the classic low-noise arrangement) exists precisely to trade efficiency for cleanliness.

## FAQ

### What is an LDO cross-reference?

An LDO cross-reference is a lookup that maps a linear regulator part number to alternative parts with comparable indexed specifications, typically output voltage, output current, package and dropout. It produces a candidate list for evaluation. It does not verify loop stability, quiescent current, thermal derating or noise performance, all of which determine whether a candidate will actually work.

### Can I replace a tantalum output capacitor with a ceramic one on an old LDO?

Often not without checking. Many older linear regulators using a PNP pass element require a minimum equivalent series resistance — typically in the region of 0.1 to 2 ohms — to keep the control loop stable, and a low-ESR ceramic can push the loop into oscillation. Check the datasheet's ESR stability plot before changing capacitor technology, and if the plot exists, treat both its upper and lower boundaries as real limits.

### Why does my LDO oscillate only at low temperature or on some units?

This is the signature of a marginal stability margin rather than an outright design error. Ceramic capacitance falls at temperature extremes and varies unit to unit, so a design sitting near the edge of the regulator's stable region passes at room temperature on most boards and fails at cold start on a few. Verify effective capacitance at the operating bias and across the full temperature range rather than relying on the printed value.

### How much dropout headroom does an LDO need?

Enough that the regulator stays out of dropout at minimum input voltage, maximum load and minimum operating temperature simultaneously. Dropout is normally specified at maximum load and 25 °C and generally worsens at low temperature, so the datasheet headline is an optimistic figure. Battery-powered designs are the tight case: a 3.3 V rail from a single-cell lithium battery near end of discharge may have only about 100 mV of headroom available.

### Does a smaller package with the same current rating work the same?

No. The current rating assumes a thermal environment. A SOT-23-5 has roughly three times the junction-to-ambient thermal resistance of a SOT-223, so the same dissipation produces a far larger temperature rise. Dissipating 0.85 W is workable in a SOT-223 at room ambient and impossible in a SOT-23-5, even though both may be marketed at the same output current.

### How do I compare PSRR between two LDOs?

Compare the curves at the frequency that matters in your system, which is usually the switching frequency of the converter upstream. Headline PSRR figures are typically quoted at 100 Hz or 1 kHz and can overstate rejection at hundreds of kilohertz by 20 dB or more. Establish the ripple amplitude you need to suppress and the tolerance of the load, convert that to a required rejection in dB, and read both datasheets at that frequency.

### Should I replace an obsolete LDO with a switching regulator?

Consider it when the input-to-output differential has grown large enough that linear dissipation is the limiting factor, and the load is not especially noise-sensitive. Converting 12 V to 3.3 V at 500 mA dissipates around 4.35 W in a linear regulator, which no small package can handle, while a buck converter would dissipate a small fraction of that. Keep the rail linear when it feeds an ADC reference, RF stage or precision analogue front end.
